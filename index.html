01 · The starting point

Consent belongs to the person

A signature is evidence of a conversation, not a substitute for one. In forensic work, the patient may also be a survivor, an accused person, a detainee, a child, or someone whose body or records may become evidence. The clinical duty to respect dignity and choice continues alongside lawful investigation.

REMEMBER 01

Separate purposes

Clinical care, medico-legal examination, specimen collection, photography, police intimation and research are distinct decisions. Explain and record them separately where relevant.

REMEMBER 02

No coercion

Police presence, family pressure, custody or urgency can undermine voluntariness. Create privacy and check that the person can say no.

REMEMBER 03

Keep helping

Declining a forensic examination does not mean declining treatment. Offer clinically needed care and explain available options.

02 · Core standard

What makes consent valid?

In Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda (2008), the Supreme Court described real and valid consent as consent from a competent person, given voluntarily, on adequate information. In Yash Charitable Trust v. Union of India (2026 INSC 96), the Court reiterated patient autonomy and the Samira Kohli framework.

Capacity

The person can understand relevant information, appreciate reasonably foreseeable consequences, and communicate a choice. Support communication; do not equate disability, diagnosis or an “unwise” choice with incapacity.

Voluntariness

Choice is free from force, threats, deception or undue influence. Explain that care is available regardless of whether the person agrees to evidence collection or a police case, subject to legally required reporting.

Adequate information

Explain nature, purpose, expected benefits/effects, reasonable alternatives, substantial risks, and likely consequences of refusal. Use language and aids the person understands; invite questions.

Specific and ongoing

Consent is limited to what was explained and agreed. A broad “all procedures” form does not authorize an unforeseen invasive or materially different procedure. Recheck consent as the examination proceeds.

Clinical rule of thumb:

For an intervention beyond the agreed scope, stop and seek specific consent. The narrow emergency exception in case law concerns immediate necessity to save life or preserve health when the patient cannot consent and no representative is available; it is not a shortcut for convenience or forensic evidence collection.

03 · A usable bedside sequence

Ask in a way that makes “no” possible

  1. Prepare privacy. Introduce yourself and role; offer a chaperone/interpreter and support person as appropriate. In custody, speak without police in the room when feasible.
  2. Explain each part. State what you propose, why, what body areas or samples are involved, what may be uncomfortable, who may receive the report/evidence, and what alternatives exist.
  3. Check understanding. Ask the person to describe in their own words what will happen. Correct misunderstandings without pressure.
  4. Ask for separate choices. For example: examination, particular sample(s), clinical photography, release/transfer of samples, and treatment. Do not bundle unrelated purposes.
  5. Agree a stop signal. Tell the person they may pause or withdraw agreement at any point, subject to explaining any immediate clinical implications.
  6. Record and proceed proportionately. Note the information given, questions, choices, limits, interpreter/chaperone, and time. Collect only what is relevant and authorized or specifically required by law.

05 · Capacity, age, refusal

Capacity is decision-specific

Mental healthcare decisions

The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 §4 presumes capacity and defines it through understanding relevant information, appreciating foreseeable consequences and communicating a decision, with support such as simple language or visual aids.

Children and adolescents

Do not use one blanket rule for every procedure. Identify the governing statute and age threshold for that decision, explain at the child’s level, seek assent where possible, and involve the legally appropriate representative without disregarding the child’s views or safety.

Refusal

Respect a capable person’s refusal. Explore concerns, clarify consequences and offer alternatives. Record the exact step refused and any partial consent. Never retaliate by withholding unrelated care.

Emergency

Provide urgently necessary care where legally justified when the person cannot decide and delay risks serious harm. Document why the person could not consent, why delay was unsafe, what was done, and when capacity/representatives were reassessed.

Remember:

“Mentally ill”, intoxicated, distressed, unconscious, in custody, or under 18 are not interchangeable legal categories. Assess the actual decision and use the specific statute that governs it.

06 · The record

Write down the conversation, not just the signature

  • Date, start/end time, place, name and role of the person obtaining consent.
  • Identity and decision-specific capacity assessment; communication support/interpreter and language used.
  • Information given: nature, purpose, steps, relevant risks, alternatives, limits, recipients/use of evidence and consequences of refusal.
  • Each separate choice: agreed, declined, deferred, withdrawn or not applicable. Capture the scope precisely.
  • Questions asked, answers, teach-back, chaperone/support person, and any pressure or safety concern.
  • For statutory authority instead of consent, record the exact legal provision/order, person authorizing, scope, safeguards and steps taken to inform the individual.
  • Signatures/marks where appropriate, witness details, copies offered, and any refusal form. A signature alone does not cure a poor process.

Use the current approved institutional form as a memory aid. Do not copy patient identifiers into teaching materials.

07 · Case room

Pause. Choose. Explain.

CASE A · PARTIAL REFUSAL

“I agree to treatment, not swabs.”

An adult survivor accepts injury care but declines genital sampling. Best response: provide care, explain the option and possible evidentiary implications without pressure, document refusal of that component, and follow applicable reporting duties.

CASE B · SCOPE CREEP

“The form says examination.”

During a limited examination, a clinician wants to take an additional intimate sample not discussed. Best response: pause, explain the sample and its purpose, obtain specific agreement or do not take it unless a clear legal authority applies.

CASE C · POLICE IN ROOM

“We need the statement now.”

A person in custody appears to agree while an officer answers for them. Best response: create a private, safe opportunity to communicate; explain choices directly; assess capacity and voluntariness; follow statutory safeguards.

CASE D · UNCONSCIOUS PATIENT

“Can we collect evidence anyway?”

Urgent treatment is needed and the person cannot decide. Best response: address immediate clinical needs under the applicable emergency basis. Do not assume that emergency treatment authority automatically authorizes non-urgent forensic evidence collection.
